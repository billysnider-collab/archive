01

The CFR loop — how solvers actually learn

Counterfactual Regret Minimization doesn't solve a game tree in one shot. It plays itself, over and over — millions of hands — and adjusts a little after each one. The loop is the whole algorithm:

[ Current strategy σᵗ ] │ ▼ [ Play many hands / traverse the tree ] │ ▼ [ Measure how much better each alternative action would have been ] │ ▼ [ Accumulate that "regret" ] │ ▼ [ Regret matching → nudge frequencies toward the high-regret actions ] │ └──────────────► repeat, millions of times

At each decision point, the agent asks a hypothetical: if it had taken a different action here, everything else held fixed, how much more would it have made? If the answer is "more," that action's regret score goes up, and it gets played more often next time. Actions with negative regret get suppressed.

Why this converges: as the average regret at every decision point drifts toward zero, the running average of all strategies played — not the most recent one, the average — converges toward a Game Theory Optimal equilibrium.

For the curious — the formal regret term \[ R_T(I, K) = \sum_{t=1}^T \pi_{-i}^{\sigma^t}(I)\Big[u_i(\sigma^t_{I \to K}, I) - u_i(\sigma^t, I)\Big] \]
$I$ — the decision point (information set)
$K$ — the alternative action being scored
$\pi_{-i}^{\sigma^t}(I)$ — probability of reaching $I$ at all, given the player is trying to
the bracket — utility with $K$ played, minus utility with the actual strategy

CFR+, the version that solved heads-up limit hold'em, made two changes: it floors negative regret at zero instead of letting it accumulate forever, so a previously-bad action can recover the moment it becomes good again — and it weights later iterations more heavily, since the strategy has had time to shake out its initial noise.

02

Why GTO's guarantees vanish at 3+ players unsolved

In heads-up, zero-sum poker, the Minimax Theorem is a genuine safety net: play the equilibrium and your opponent — no matter what they do — cannot push your expected return below zero. That guarantee is a two-player, zero-sum artifact. Add a third player and it disappears.

  • No proven convergence. CFR has no guarantee of reaching a stable equilibrium once there are three or more players.
  • Multiple, disjoint equilibria. There isn't one GTO to converge to — there can be several.
  • Equity doesn't have to go to you. A balanced strategy defends against individual, uncoordinated deviation — not against a chaotic multi-way pot.

"If two players adopt uncoordinated or reckless sub-optimal strategies, they can dramatically increase game variance and bleed chips to each other — in ways that inadvertently destroy the expected value of the third, 'GTO' player."

the Rock-Maniac dynamic

A wildly loose "maniac" shoving every hand has to lose that equity to somebody — but nothing forces it toward the disciplined GTO player. It often gets swallowed by whichever opponent adjusts fastest, not by whoever is most theoretically correct. Playing a textbook-balanced strategy in a three-way pot can, in practice, lose EV to a player who's simply reading the table better in the moment.

03

Taming continuous bet sizes

Limit hold'em has a handful of fixed bet sizes, so its tree can be written down explicitly. No-limit doesn't — a bet can be any amount from one chip to a player's entire stack. That's an infinite action space, and solvers can't enumerate infinity.

  • Action abstraction: restrict every decision point to a small set of representative sizes — say 33%, 75%, and 150% pot, plus all-in. If a real opponent bets something off that list, the solver interpolates and maps it onto the nearest branch it actually solved for.
  • Card bucketing: hands with near-identical equity and similar board texture get grouped into a shared bucket, especially on later streets, compressing a state space that would otherwise run into the terabytes.

Using suit symmetry alone, the raw 22,100 possible flops collapse to 455 canonical structures — monotone, two-tone, and rainbow textures that are strategically identical up to relabeling suits. That kind of shortcut is most of where the tractability comes from.

04

How humans actually use this at the table

A solver hands a player the destination: a set of balanced frequencies. A human still has to get there in real time, with a fraction of a solver's memory and none of its patience. In practice, strong players approximate the theory with a few shortcuts:

  • Range thinking, not hand thinking. "What does my whole range look like from here?" rather than "is this specific hand good?"
  • Frequency anchors. A rough rule like "bluff about a third as often as I value-bet on this texture" — close enough without recomputing from scratch each hand.
  • Blockers as soft information. Holding the A♠ makes a nut-flush bluff less likely from the opponent — real signal, but easy to over-weight if it's the only thing you're looking at.
  • Default to balance, deviate on evidence. Start near GTO, and only step off it once a specific opponent shows a repeated, provable leak.

The solver is the map. The player is still the one driving, reading traffic no map saw coming.

05

Cross-domain: poker ↔ quantitative trading

Quant firms recruit poker players because the two disciplines run on the same underlying math: probabilistic decisions, made under incomplete information, against an adversary who is also trying to win.

Poker conceptTrading analogue
Hidden hole cardsHidden order-book liquidity, dark pool prints
Card removal / blockersOrder cancellations, volume imbalance as a "removal effect" on hidden flow
Balanced value-bet / bluff mixSetting a bid-ask spread that can't be picked off
Reading a calling stationTrading against toxic, predictable order flow
Enduring variance over 25M handsJudging edge over thousands of trades, not one

The common thread: separate the short-term, noisy outcome from the long-run, statistically real edge — and don't let one bad beat, or one bad trade, convince you the underlying strategy was wrong.

In one line
Solvers work by obsessively tracking what would have been better at every decision point, then slowly adjusting until neither side can improve by changing — abstraction is what makes that computable, and multiplayer dynamics are what keep the real game unsolved.
