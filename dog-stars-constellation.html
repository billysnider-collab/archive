Nine years out

How the world ended

Not one collapse but three — a hyper-virulent flu, a second autoimmune sickness born from the first, and a climate already failing underneath both.

01
The Flu
An airborne strain kills — by Hig's count — "ninety nine point whatever" percent in a matter of weeks. The press blamed New Delhi. Cima, the epidemiologist, has heard the other rumor: a genetic accident at the Livermore weapons lab, a courier's plane down in Brampton. No one ever confirmed either story.
02
The Blood Disease
Survivors develop a slow autoimmune condition, "something like AIDS" — bruising, hemorrhage, organ damage. Children born after the pandemic inherit it. There is no cure. Cima suspects vitamin D deficiency accelerates it.
03
The Land Itself
Underneath both plagues the climate was already failing. Warming streams killed the trout. Drought and beetles killed the forests. Snowpack thinned until whole canyons dried. Elk, geese, tigers, apes — gone. Only the adaptable remain: carp, coyotes, raptors, and the people still stubborn enough to farm.
04
Why survivors turned feral
No law, no courts, no hospitals — only scarcity and the people it makes of us. Bangley's doctrine, "New world now. New world new rules," is the logic almost everyone left alive has adopted. Trust became a luxury no one could afford. The men at the Coke truck, the marauders on the plains, the couple at Grand Junction who rigged a radio beacon to lure pilots into a cable trap — all of them are the product of that logic.
Constellations

Who endures

Each a star in a broken sky, bound by grief, love, and the stubborn will to keep going.

✈︎
Hig
Pilot · Narrator
A poet, carpenter, and reluctant killer. He flies the Beast, tends his garden, and carries the weight of a world he could not save. The End did not harden him into a survivalist; it hollowed him into someone still capable of killing while still carrying pre-apocalypse ethics that have nowhere left to go. That gap is the wound the whole book keeps returning to.
I keep the Beast running, I keep the 100 low lead on tap, I foresee attacks. I am young enough, I am old enough.
⚔
Bangley
Survivalist · Protector
Ruthless, pragmatic, and utterly alone. He lives for the perimeter, kills without hesitation, and guards Hig like a difficult brother. His philosophy is pure: never negotiate, trust no one, protect the perimeter at all costs. He is effective. He is also nearly killed alone behind a tool chest, and the novel makes clear that his way keeps you alive but does not make life worth living.
Never ever negotiate. You are negotiating your own death.
🐾
Jasper
Copilot · Heart
Hig's blue heeler, the last living witness to before. Copilot, bed-mate, the one creature who still needed and loved Hig the way things used to be loved. His death in the mountains is the turning point of the novel: with his last anchor removed, Hig is free to risk everything chasing a voice on the radio.
Goodbye, bud. You are Jasper. My heart. We are never apart, not here, not there.
🌿
Cima
Doctor · Healer
A survivor who carries her own losses — a twin brother, a husband she had to kill. She meets Hig in their shared brokenness. Their relationship is not a replacement for Melissa; it is proof that a person who has lost everything can still make room for someone new. That is a smaller, more honest kind of hope than survival stories usually allow.
If you want, you can just look at me. It's probably been a long time. I'm in no hurry.
🕊
Melissa
Memory · Love
Hig's wife, lost to the flu. He smothered her at her request — the act that haunts him more than any other. She is the love he lost, the pregnancy that never came, the home that can never be returned to. Everything Hig does afterward is shaped by that moment.
I loved her. This is what I live with.
🏔
Pops
Rancher · Father
Cima's father, a former Navy SEAL who carries the old West in his bones. Dignified, weary, and quietly hopeful. He and Bangley, once potential enemies, become allies. The green patches, he says, are spreading year by year.
The green patches are spreading year by year.
The flight log

Past the point of no return

Hig's route across Colorado in three books — from the fortified perimeter at Erie, through grief and ambush, to a hidden canyon and back again. Jasper's death removes his last reason for caution. The radio voice offers something the base can never give him: the possibility that connection still exists.

Erie — Coke Truck — Mountains — Grand Junction — The Canyon — Home
Book One
Life on the Perimeter
  • Hig and Bangley hold the Erie airport. Hig flies patrol in the Beast; Bangley mans the sniper tower. An uneasy alliance keeps them both alive.
  • Hig secretly runs supplies to a Mennonite community dying of the blood disease, against Bangley's warnings.
  • A mysterious radio transmission reaches him from Grand Junction — a voice he puts off chasing for years.
  • On a last hunting trip into the mountains, Jasper dies in his sleep. Hig is left with nothing but the stars.
Book Two
The Journey West
  • Grieving and unmoored, Hig is ambushed by nine marauders on the plains. Bangley saves him with mortars fired from the perimeter.
  • Hig finally flies past his "point of no return," discovering a hidden box canyon with a stone house, a garden, and cattle.
  • He meets Cima, a doctor, and her father Pops — hostile at first, then cautiously trusting.
  • Hig and Cima fall into a tender relationship. Pops reveals the canyon's creek is drying up; the three plan to leave together.
Book Three
Return and Reckoning
  • The radio transmission from Grand Junction turns out to be a trap — an elderly couple luring pilots into a cable strung across the runway. Pops kills them.
  • Back at Erie they find Bangley's house destroyed. He's alive behind a tool chest, having fought off fourteen attackers alone. Cima nurses him back.
  • The four become a household — Hig, Bangley, Cima, and Pops — an apocalyptic version of a family.
  • Unidentified jets, crews speaking Arabic, pass overhead. The novel ends open — other survivors, other threats, the world still turning.
The arc of the sky

Themes that burn

Loss, memory, violence, hope — the novel's constellations are carved from what remains when civilization falls away.

Grief as an element
"Grief is an element. It has its own cycle like the carbon cycle, the nitrogen. It never diminishes not ever."

Hig carries grief the way the earth carries water — it cycles through him, never disappearing, only changing form. Jasper's death does not end it; it only makes the cycle more visible.
Survival vs. living
Bangley survives. Hig lives.

The novel asks the question Hig cannot answer when Cima's father presses him: why fly past the point of no return? Jasper's death removed his last reason for caution. The radio voice offered something the base could never give him. He was looking for a reason to keep being human, not just alive.
The collapse of nature
"If I ever woke up crying in the middle of a dream… it's because the trout are gone every one."

The warming streams, the dying forests, the vanishing elk — the apocalypse is ecological as much as human. Yet the green patches are spreading. Spruce and aspen return. Hawks and eagles flourish. Nature is not entirely dead; it is selective in what it keeps.
Memory as home
"I lift my head off the pillow / I see the frost the moon. / Lowering my head I think of home."

Home is not a place. It is Melissa, Jasper, the life before. Hig carries them in every breath. The novel is bookended by Chinese poems about exile and reunion — Li Po at the opening, Li Shang-Yin at the close.
Violence & compassion
"Try to do the right thing. Circumstance intervenes."

Hig kills because he must, but never without cost. The novel refuses to resolve the tension — both cruelty and tenderness are real, and both are choices. Human nature is not fixed; it is a spectrum the circumstances reveal.
Found family
By the end, Hig has a family again.

Cima, Pops, and even Bangley become something like a household — an apocalyptic parody of Norman Rockwell, but real. The transactional alliance between Hig and Bangley becomes mutual care. Community is the novel's final affirmation.

Hig explains why he flies — not freedom, but the way the wrecked world resolves into pattern from above. The Garden

The final word

When will I be home?

The novel closes with Li Shang-Yin's poem — a meditation on longing, reunion, and the hope that burns even in the darkest night. It answers the novel's opening question without resolving it.

When Will I Be Home?
— Li Shang-Yin (Tang Dynasty)
When will I be home? I don't know.
In the mountains, in the rainy night,
The Autumn lake is flooded.
Someday we will be back together again.
We will sit in the candlelight by the West window.
And I will tell you how I remembered you
Tonight on the stormy mountain.
"The love is the creek bed and the pain fills it. Fills it every day with tears."
— Hig, on the nature of grief
