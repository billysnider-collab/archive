Élan — Chapter I

The Burial Pit

Pages 7–10 · Subject: Cyril Bagger

Summary

Argonne Forest, October 1918, fourteen days into the offensive. Cyril Bagger, a burial-duty soldier in Company P, is dug out of the mass grave he'd been digging when a mortar hit it. Everyone near him is dead; he's untouched, and refuses to call it a miracle. The front has gone quiet and the army has withdrawn east, leaving his company behind. He pulls out his father's old Bible, not for scripture but for the smell — it steadies him. Clearing blood and mud from his ears, he hears an unearthly shriek: organic, unidentifiable as human or animal, dying slow, dying loud. The chapter ends unresolved, right on that sound.

Personnel

  • Cyril BaggerBurial duty by choice. Son of Bishop Bernard Bagger — the Bible is sensory comfort, not faith. Fourteen days in, he can distinguish types of blood by taste and smell. Copes with crude humor and denial, and actively rejects the word "miracle" for his own survival.

Intercepted fragments

"he'll be goddamned if he's going to start believing in miracles here in hell"
the shriek — "it could be male or female, human or animal, but whatever it is, it's dying, dying slow, dying loud"
the war as "a sentence in a book careening without periods... a terrible black wheel"

Analysis

Style — one continuous run-on sentence structure mirrors the ceaseless, breathless quality of trench warfare; the closing line makes this explicit as metaphor.
Miracle vs. denial — Bagger survives against all odds and refuses the miraculous framing. Worth tracking against the title.
Faith as body memory — religion shows up as smell and nostalgia, not belief, suggesting a fracture with his father.
Desensitization — the "sommelier palate" for blood types marks how far removed he already is from his old self.

Unresolved

  • Does the shriek get identified or explained in Chapter II?
  • Is Bagger's survival ever explained — miracle, coincidence, or something else?
  • Watch how "miracle" language recurs through the book.
  • How does the title Angel Down connect to a soldier who should be dead and isn't?

Élan — Chapter II

The Man with the Tiny Arm

Pages 11–17 · Subjects: Bagger, Arno

Summary

Bagger wipes what he thinks is brain matter from his face — it's clay. He notices a voice beneath the shriek, calling his name, and dismisses it as shellshock. He checks on Company P, steadies himself with an imagined Pershing pep-talk, then drifts into his backstory: a career con man from Iowa who's spent the war rigging other soldiers into taking his combat risks. He resents and secretly loves Lewis Arno, a fourteen-year-old who lied about his age to enlist. Arno finds him, asks if he's dead, and points out something stuck to Bagger's face — a human ear, not his own. Bagger jokes it off, shaken. Arno then delivers the real shock: the major general himself — described only as "the man with the tiny little arm" — is asking for Bagger by name. The chapter ends with Bagger dizzy, panicked, nearly collapsing into the grave.

Personnel

  • Cyril BaggerBackstory revealed as a professional con artist off the Mississippi riverboats. His entire war strategy: manipulate other soldiers into combat duty so he can stay on burial detail. States outright he cares for no one — a claim the text immediately undercuts.
  • Lewis ArnoFourteen years old, an underage enlistee attached to the 172nd. Small, unskilled, "too small for the Germans to hit." The one person Bagger can't bring himself to fully swindle or harden against.
  • The major general"The man with the tiny little arm" — introduced only through Arno's confused description. His interest in a lowly PFC alarms Bagger, who assumes his cons have finally caught up with him.

Intercepted fragments

the voice says, Bagger — "it's stimulating how the word seems to buzz from within his own flesh"
"he's just a guy who's going to get through this war by living how he's always lived, not giving one shit about anyone"

Analysis

The con man as survival strategy — Bagger's identity is built on manufactured personas and manipulation. Watch how that collides with something that can't be conned.
The voice vs. the shriek — two distinct threads now: the wordless dying shriek from Chapter I, and a voice that specifically says his name.
Arno as the crack in the armor — deliberately positioned as Bagger's one soft spot.
Withheld information — the major general is introduced entirely through a confused child's fragment, building dread through partial description rather than exposition.

Unresolved

  • Is the voice saying "Bagger" connected to the shriek, or separate?
  • Who is the major general — and why does he want a PFC by name?
  • Does Bagger's con-man past set up something he can't swindle his way out of?
  • Whose ear was it, and does it come up again?

Élan — Chapter III

Three Becomes Five

Pages 18–20 · Subjects: Bagger, Arno, Company P

Summary

A short, tense chapter. Bagger and Arno walk together while Arno chatters about The Son of Tarzan — it turns out Bagger has secretly been reading pulp adventure novels aloud to the illiterate Arno for some time, spicing them up with invented filth, and has become genuinely invested despite himself. Amid the banter, Bagger quietly shields Arno from horrors along the path. They rejoin the remains of Company P, forming up to march under Lieutenant Aquila and Sergeant DiStefano, with Father Muensterman nearby. Arno pulls Bagger instead into a shell crater — and there they find three men standing at rigid attention, weapons ready. Bagger recognizes them as the company's three most expendable men. Then the gut-punch: it isn't three men standing there. It's five.

Personnel

  • Cyril BaggerRevealed to have been reading to Arno for a long stretch of the war — the "biggest mistake" he's made, by his own admission, and he keeps making it. Shields Arno from gore twice in this chapter, then denies the instinct.
  • Lewis ArnoIlliterate — confessed this only to Bagger. Genuinely happy amid the horror, latched onto pulp fiction as an escape. Possibly complicit in "playing the role Bagger needs."
  • Supporting castLieutenant Jules Aquila, giving hollow pep talks. Sergeant Moss DiStefano, passing smelling salts. Father Muensterman, the company chaplain, reduced to vomiting into his own hands.

Intercepted fragments

reading pulp novels aloud is "bar none the biggest mistake Bagger's made in the Army, and worse still, he keeps making it"
"it isn't the three most disposable gathered here but the five"

Analysis

Tenderness disguised as cruelty — the Bagger–Arno bond deepens through action, directly contradicting Bagger's stated indifference in Chapter II.
Performed normalcy — the faked "hole" moment shows two traumatized people collaborating on a fiction to survive the day.
The uncanny creeps in from the edges — mostly tender, then a single-word button line — three becoming five — recontextualizes the whole scene.

Unresolved

  • Who are the other two men, and why does the count matter?
  • Is this tied to the shriek or the voice — or something new?
  • Will Bagger's growing attachment to Arno become a liability later?
  • Track the rhythm: banter, gore, punchline, gut-punch — does it repeat?
